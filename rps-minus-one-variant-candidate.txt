RPS MINUS ONE - VARIANT CANDIDATE

STATUS

Strong candidate for the nine-game lineup.

This variant keeps normal RPS recognizable while adding a meaningful second
decision. Its unique mechanic is staged information: players first choose a
pair of possible moves, reveal those possibilities, and then secretly choose
which move to keep.


RULES

The game is played first to 6 pips.

Scoring:

- A win awards 2 pips to the winner.
- A tie awards 1 pip to each player.
- If both players reach 6 pips together, play sudden-death standard RPS.
- In sudden death, tied throws repeat until one player wins.

Each round has up to two phases.

PHASE ONE: CHOOSE A PAIR

Each player secretly chooses one of three pairs:

- Rock + Paper
- Paper + Scissors
- Scissors + Rock

The pairs reveal simultaneously.

If both players chose the same pair, skip phase two and award both players
1 pip. Start a new round.

Duplicate pairs such as Rock + Rock are not available. They remove the second
decision and add no useful play.

PHASE TWO: CHOOSE A MOVE TO KEEP

If the revealed pairs differ, each player sees all four available moves. Each
player secretly chooses one move from their own pair to keep. The kept moves
reveal simultaneously and resolve as standard RPS.

- Different moves: winner gains 2 pips.
- Same move: both players gain 1 pip.

UI should ask which move to KEEP, not which move to remove.


PAIR RELATIONSHIP

The phase-one pairs form their own RPS-like cycle:

- Rock + Paper has advantage over Scissors + Rock.
- Scissors + Rock has advantage over Paper + Scissors.
- Paper + Scissors has advantage over Rock + Paper.

"Advantage" does not mean an automatic win. It means the advantaged player
has the stronger phase-two subgame.

The phase-one UI can show the three pairs as three buttons with arrows showing
this cycle, matching the visual language of the other variants.


WHY MATCHING PAIRS SCORE AS A TIE

When both players reveal the same pair, one move weakly dominates the other.

Example: Rock + Paper versus Rock + Paper.

- Keeping Paper against Paper ties.
- Keeping Paper against Rock wins.
- Keeping Rock against Paper loses.
- Keeping Rock against Rock ties.

Both rational players keep Paper, producing a tie. Phase two contains no
meaningful decision in this case, so the game resolves it immediately and
awards 1 pip each.

Redoing phase one instead would make many rounds produce no progress and would
allow deliberate stalling.


PHASE-TWO STRATEGY

Example advantaged matchup:

Player A: Rock + Paper
Player B: Scissors + Rock

Player A's Rock is the safe move:

- Rock versus Rock ties.
- Rock versus Scissors wins.

The safe move is not always optimal. If Player A always keeps Rock, Player B
can always keep Rock and force a tie. Player A must sometimes keep Paper to
punish that response. Player B must sometimes keep Scissors to punish Paper.

Equilibrium strategy for this matchup:

Player A, advantaged:

- Keep safe Rock: 2/3
- Keep risky Paper: 1/3

Player B, disadvantaged:

- Keep shared Rock: 2/3
- Keep counter Scissors: 1/3

Immediate outcome probabilities:

- Advantaged player wins: 4/9
- Tie: 4/9
- Disadvantaged player wins: 1/9

With a tie valued as half a win, the advantaged player's value is 2/3. The
advantaged player wins four times as often as the disadvantaged player, but
ties remain common.

Across the complete game, optimal phase-one play chooses each pair 1/3 of the
time. The complete game is fair: both players have equal overall value.


WHY PHASE-TWO TIES DO NOT REPLAY

Repeating only phase two creates pathological play.

With different pairs, the advantaged player can keep the safe move almost all
the time and occasionally use the risky move. This can push their eventual win
probability arbitrarily close to 100%, potentially after many ties.

With matching pairs, rational players can repeat the dominant shared move
forever, creating a permanent deadlock.

Awarding 1 pip to each player avoids both problems.


MATCH LENGTH

Every scored round distributes exactly 2 total pips:

- A decisive round gives 2 to one player.
- A tied round gives 1 to each player.

Therefore a match lasts at least 3 Minus One rounds and reaches a result or a
6-6 tiebreak after no more than 6 Minus One rounds.

With evenly mixed pair choices, matching pairs occur about 1/3 of the time and
skip phase two. A six-round match therefore averages approximately 10 player
decisions: 6 pair selections plus about 4 keep selections.

This should put the variant near standard RPS first-to-five in total input
count, but each Minus One decision requires more thought.


WHY IT FITS THE PACK

- Standard RPS provides pure reads.
- Fireball War adds state and resources.
- RPS RPG adds persistent progression.
- The positioning game adds spatial decisions.
- RPS Minus One adds staged information.

It is culturally recognizable, easy to present with the existing RPS UI
language, mathematically fair, and strategically unintuitive in a promising
way. A player can identify a safe move without that move automatically being
optimal.


PLAYTEST QUESTIONS

- Does awarding pips make ties feel productive rather than anticlimactic?
- Is first to 6 the right length in the full ranked-match sequence?
- Does skipping phase two on matching pairs feel clean and immediate?
- Can players understand the pair-advantage arrows without needing math?
- Does the safe-versus-risky phase-two decision become intuitive through play?
- Does sudden-death standard RPS feel like a satisfying finish at 6-6?
